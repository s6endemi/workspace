Why Your First Hundred Metres In The Cold Feel Different

On a cold morning your first hundred metres belong to a different body than the rest of the walk. Your body moves best within a narrow temperature range, and cold air pulls it under that within minutes, in your legs and hands. Nothing has been lost, it simply hasn't caught up yet.

Picture yourself stepping out the front door on a frosty morning for a walk. Your first strides come out short and careful, knees barely bending, and by the end of the block your steps have loosened into your normal easy stride, like a switch flipped somewhere along the way.

And here's the part almost nobody notices: the same thing happens when you climb out of a cold car in a car park, your legs feel locked for a few steps before they loosen up. It happens again in a cold garage early in the morning, when clearing tools into a box feels slow and heavy for the first few minutes. Both times, nothing is wrong, your body is running a few degrees behind the room.

None of this means your body is falling apart in the cold, it just needs a small head start before it moves the way you expect. So here's the payoff: on cold mornings, spend two minutes doing slow easy movement indoors, marching in place or gentle knee bends, before you step outside.
