## Script source

Nobody tells you why a short flight can make your body feel years older.

It can start before the seatbelt sign is off. Your hips feel stuck when you stand in the aisle. Your ankles feel strangely stiff at the gate. You bend to lift your bag and your low back wants to negotiate. Then you get to the hotel, turn in bed, and wonder why a day of sitting left you feeling like you ran a race.

That does not mean your body suddenly became fragile. A plane seat asks you to hold a very small range for a long time. Your hips stay bent, your ankles barely move, and your trunk stays braced against the seat. When you stand, those areas have to restart their conversation fast. It is like closing a pocket umbrella for hours, then expecting it to open smoothly the first time you need it.

PREVIA's free movement scan can show how your ankles, hips, and trunk share motion in simple movements. It gives you a clearer place to start than blaming one stiff area after every trip.

Try this at the gate or when you get to your room. Stand facing a wall with both hands on it. Keep one heel down and slowly bring that knee toward the wall ten times, then switch. Next, hold the back of a chair, step one foot behind you, and gently bend the front knee while keeping the back heel heavy for twenty seconds each side. Finish standing tall with your hands on your ribs and slowly rotate your chest to the left and right five times without moving your feet.

Which part feels most different after a flight: your hips, ankles, or low back? Put it in the comments. That answer tells a more useful story than calling the whole trip uncomfortable.
