Your heels did not get tight. Chairs trained them to stop bending.

Try to drop into a deep squat for ten seconds. If your heels lift, your knees fold inward, or your chest falls forward, it is easy to blame age.

That story is too convenient.

Your ankle needs the shin to travel forward over the foot. But chairs, car seats, and raised-heel shoes almost never ask for that range. Day after day, your body becomes efficient at the positions it repeats. Then one day you try to squat, and your ankles act like the movement belongs to somebody else.

You can see the contrast in a toddler. They sit on the floor, squat to grab a toy, stand up, and repeat it without a warm-up. They did not build a special mobility routine. They never stopped visiting the position.

With PREVIA, you can test where the chain changes first, whether it is the ankle, hip, or the way your trunk shifts to get around both. That makes the deep squat a useful self-test, not a pass-or-fail trick.

Bring it back in small pieces. Face a wall, keep one heel down, and guide each knee toward the wall for ten slow reps. Hold a book in front of you as a counterweight and sit into a supported squat for 30 seconds. Later, spend two minutes on the floor instead of another stretch on the sofa.

You are not building a new body. You are revisiting a position your body used to know.

The chair did not ruin you. It simply made one useful movement optional for too long.
