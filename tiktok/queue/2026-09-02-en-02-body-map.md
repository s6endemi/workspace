Your body gets worse at knowing where it is when it never has to adjust

There is a map in your brain that tracks where your joints are without you looking. You notice it only when it gets a little fuzzy.

You stand up after a long desk day and one leg feels oddly slow. You turn to grab something behind you and your back moves as one solid piece. You catch your shoulder on a doorway you have walked through a thousand times. Stairs feel less natural than they should. None of that means your body is broken. It can mean your day gave it almost nothing new to map.

Your balance system learns through tiny adjustments. A foot shifts on uneven ground. Your ribs turn when you reach. One hip takes weight while the other leg swings. Sit in one position, look at one screen, move along the same flat routes, and those adjustments get rare. Your body still has the map. It is just using a very small part of it.

PREVIA's free movement scan picks up the places where your body avoids loading or turning. It takes five minutes on your phone.

Try a quick reset later. Stand barefoot or in flat shoes and slowly shift your weight from one foot to the other for thirty seconds. Take five slow turns in each direction, letting your eyes find a point on the wall. Then walk across the room with a deliberately longer back step. No workout required. You are feeding your balance system information it has not had all day.

Most people notice the difference as a little more ease, not some dramatic transformation. That is enough. The goal is to make ordinary movement feel familiar again instead of making every turn a surprise.

## Caption
A desk day does more than make you stiff. It can make ordinary movement feel less automatic because your body has spent hours using the same tiny range of positions. A few slow weight shifts, turns, and a real walk can give that system more to work with. Keep this for the next day you feel clumsy for no obvious reason.
