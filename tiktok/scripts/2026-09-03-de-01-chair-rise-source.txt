Standing up from a chair should not feel like a test

If you need a second before standing up, if your first few steps feel stiff, or if your low back complains every time you leave the car, people usually blame age or a weak back.

The surprising part is that rising from a chair is not a back move. It is a whole lower body handoff. Your ankles need to travel forward. Your hips need to move behind you. Then your hips and legs bring you back up while your trunk stays balanced over them.

Chairs train the opposite position for hours. Hips fixed near ninety degrees. Ankles barely moving. Your body gets very efficient at being supported, then asks the back to help when it has to get moving again. That can make an ordinary chair rise feel strangely heavy.

This is exactly the kind of pattern a free PREVIA movement scan can make visible. It looks at how your body shares the job in movements such as a squat, so you can see where it starts compensating instead of guessing from the ache.

Try this quick reclaim today. First, sit on a firm chair and stand up slowly five times. Take three seconds up and three seconds down. Keep both feet planted and let your knees travel over your toes. Next, put one foot near a wall and gently bring the knee toward the wall ten times on each side, with the heel down. Finish with a supported deep squat hold for thirty seconds, holding a doorframe or countertop if needed.

These are not magic moves. They give your ankles, hips, and legs a chance to share a job your back has been covering for. Your body did not forget how to stand up. It has spent a long time practicing the easier version.
